# What "best" means

Each profile gives the job the material does, the criteria you score from 1 to 5, the hard gates (pass or fail), and the ways this kind of material usually fails. Add one to three criteria from the brief. Company-specific rules come from the private house rules note, never from here.

## Every profile

These checks are part of the format gate: you check them rather than score them. All of them apply to non-fiction. For stories and other creative pieces, only the last three apply.

- **Point first.** The first sentence, slide or screen says the thing. No warm-up.
- **One-sentence takeaway.** A reader could repeat the point in one sentence after one pass.
- **First sentences carry weight.** The first sentence of every section or slide says something.
- **Specific over vague.** Name the thing, give the number with its source, show the example.
- **Cut 10%.** This is a probe, not a quota. Try cutting a tenth, and keep each cut that loses nothing.
- **Read it aloud.** Anything that sounds stilted or over-formal gets rewritten.
- **No AI tells:**
  - None of "delve", "leverage", "robust", "seamless", "landscape" or "crucial".
  - No "Here's the thing", "serves as", "it's worth noting" or "In today's…".
  - Two or three em dashes per 500 words at most.
  - No reflexive lists of three in prose. Real step lists are fine, and so is a deliberate rule of three in a story.
  - One rhetorical question at most, and no closing summaries.

## Customer or sales deck

**Job:** move one reader one step: a call, a pilot, an intro, a yes.

| Criterion | A 5 looks like |
|---|---|
| One idea | One sentence the deck exists to prove, and every slide serves it. |
| Skim test | The slide titles alone, in order, tell the story. Titles are claims ("Kernels in days, not quarters"), not labels ("Results"). |
| Their world first | The reader's problem and what changed come before our solution and proof. |
| Proof | Specific, sourced and qualified. The strongest proof leads the proof section. |
| One point per slide | Three steps of complexity at most. Each slide reads in about ten seconds. |
| The ask | A concrete next step with who and when. |
| Visual | On brand, and nothing overflows, crops or shrinks to unreadable in the render. |

**Gates:** house rules. Every number sourced and qualified. No repeated slides. No takedowns of competitors. No price in writing unless the brief says so. Renders cleanly.

**A section of a deck:** "The ask" becomes the hand-off to the next section, and assume the company was named earlier in the deck. Speaker notes travel with the deck, so they follow the same gates as the slides.

**Usually fails by:** being conceptual instead of concrete. Defensive "why others fail" slides. Technical detail too early. Floor/target/stretch tiers that invite doubt. Counts that mean nothing to the reader ("1,024 positions" instead of "every token"). Fixing the PDF and not the source, so the next rebuild undoes the fix.

## One-pager or sales sheet

**Job:** let a buyer decide in 30 seconds whether to take a call.

| Criterion | A 5 looks like |
|---|---|
| Outcome headline | The headline is the buyer's outcome, not our feature. |
| What you buy | It's explicit what they are buying and what they get. |
| Their pain | Pains are in the buyer's words, not ours. |
| Proof | At least one specific, sourced proof point. |
| Call to action | It names the first step and what problem that step solves. |
| Scannable | The eye path works: headline, proof, ask. |

**Gates:** exactly one page. The render was checked. Status words are honest and sit in the sentence itself ("in pilot"), not only in a tag that won't print. House rules.

**Usually fails by:** adding lines instead of swapping them. Feature lists. Status hidden in metadata.

## Case study

**Job:** make the next buyer think "that's us, and it worked".

| Criterion | A 5 looks like |
|---|---|
| Problem | In the customer's terms, with the stakes. |
| Approach | Specific enough to believe, short enough to skim. |
| Result | Dated, scoped, quantified. |
| Customer voice | A quote that says something specific, not "great partner". |
| Transfer | Why the next reader should care. |

**Gates:** written clearance on file for the customer's name, every quote and the logo, before anything is published. Metrics match their source exactly, including dates and scope. Nothing the customer would consider confidential.

**Usually fails by:** partnership puffery. Metrics with no date or scope. A quote that was only approved out loud.

## Customer or outreach email (also Slack)

**Job:** get one reply. Slack DMs and billing or ops messages use this profile too. For Slack, use Slack's markup, and count billing or ops messages as follow-ups for length.

| Criterion | A 5 looks like |
|---|---|
| Point first | The first line is why you're writing. |
| One ask | One concrete ask (a call, a forward, a yes or no), with a time if it's a call. |
| Short | Cold outreach under about 120 words. Follow-ups under about 200. |
| Personal | Something only this reader would get. |
| Easy reply | They can answer in one line. |

**Gates:** every claim already appears in approved material (a deck or a sheet) or in the brief's sources of truth. House-rule naming. Draft only, never sent.

**Usually fails by:** thank-you padding. Listing secondary features. Two asks. Hedging openers ("I hope this finds you well").

## Investor update

**Job:** keep investors informed and get them to act on the asks.

| Criterion | A 5 looks like |
|---|---|
| Numbers first | A fixed table of the same metrics every time. |
| Bad news up front | Corrections and misses come first, in the founder's own words. |
| Highlights | Three to five one-line facts. |
| Asks | Specific enough that an investor can act today (a named intro, a role, a customer type). |
| Honest categories | Recurring and project revenue kept clearly separate. Pipeline is not signed. |

**Gates:** numbers match the current books. Confidential items stay out of the wider tier. The audience tier's rules are followed.

**Usually fails by:** waiting for good news before sending. Vanity metrics. Spin.

## Proposal or memo

**Job:** get a decision.

| Criterion | A 5 looks like |
|---|---|
| Decision first | The first paragraph says what is being decided and by when. |
| Options | Real options, with a recommendation and the reason. |
| Cost and risk | What it costs, what could go wrong, and what happens if we do nothing. |
| Structure | A busy reader can skim it in two minutes. |

**Gates:** numbers sourced. Commitments match what can actually be delivered: built is not delivered.

**Usually fails by:** burying the ask. Options that are straw men. No cost of doing nothing.

## Resume or CV

**Job:** the reader puts this person on the interview list after a ten-second skim, and the closer read backs that decision with proof.

**Readers.** A resume is read twice:
- **The screen.** A recruiter or an applicant-tracking system spends seconds on the title, the current role, the dates and the keywords.
- **The hiring manager.** They read the bullets for scope, ownership and results.

An internal move adds a reader who knows the systems. Internal shorthand is proof to that reader and noise to everyone else.

**One brief per audience.** When the same person targets two kinds of role (an internal move and an outside job, or two different job families), write a brief for each version: its reader, its job and its keywords. Both versions share one fact base. Ask for the job posting; keywords tuned to a posting beat guesses.

| Criterion | A 5 looks like |
|---|---|
| Top third | The name, a target-role line and a two- or three-line summary say what this person is for and why, with one piece of proof. A reader who stops there could pass the resume on. |
| Proof | Each bullet starts with a verb and ends on a result: scope, scale or a measured outcome, with the number's unit and basis. The ownership word matches the record (built, led, owned, contributed). |
| Fit | The most relevant work comes first and gets the most space. Older or off-target roles shrink to a line or two. The posting's keywords appear where the work happened, not only in a skills list. |
| Scannability | One column, with a clear hierarchy: name, section, employer, title, bullets. Dates sit in one aligned place. Bullets run two lines or less. There's white space between roles, and both pages are used evenly. |
| Honest punch | As strong as the record supports, and no stronger. No inflated titles, no invented metrics, and no skills the work doesn't show. A strong fact stated plainly beats an adjective. |

**Gates:**
- **Facts.** Every title, date, number and tool comes from the person's own material. A missing metric is a `needs Idan` question, never an estimate. Where versions of the record disagree, the newest one the person wrote wins, and claims it dropped stay dropped unless the person restores them.
- **Arithmetic.** Each "N years" figure matches the role dates, and the same number reads the same in every version.
- **Length.** One page early in a career, two at most otherwise. Render the PDF and count the pages; never trust the editor's estimate.
- **Machine-readable.**
	- Standard section headings, with one employer and title per entry.
	- No text in headers, footers, text boxes or images.
	- No tables or columns for core content.
	- A common font and one date format.
	- Deliver both `.docx` and PDF.
- **Confidentiality.** A version for outside readers carries no employer-confidential detail: internal program and project names, unreleased products, incident specifics, partner names, or internal figures a competitor could use. The same facts can go in plain terms.
- **Personal data.** No first person and no photo. Nothing beyond contact details (no age, marital status or full address) unless the target market expects it.
- **Privacy.** Resumes about real people stay local: the work folder and the output go in `~/Downloads`, never a shared drive or an online tool.

**Tests:**
- **Re-check every bullet you shorten against the source.** Compression is where claims drift: a "before delivery" the record never said, a cause that was only a sequence, a "submission" that was a "response". In testing, one round of tightening introduced five such drifts.
- **Render through the application the reader will use** (Word for `.docx`) and judge the pages, not the source. A font installed on only one OS reflows on the other, and a missing glyph prints as a box.
- **Balance the pages.** Cuts can empty page 2. Spend the freed space on readability (an 11 pt body, room between roles), never on restoring cuts. Keep short roles and the skills block from splitting across a page break. If the split still lands badly, an "Earlier experience" section starting on page 2 is cleaner than tuning the spacing.

**Content floor.** The person's own accomplishments are proof, not ornament. With `dieter=on`, Dieter removes only true duplicates and filler from a resume. Every fact in the person's draft stays unless the person drops it, and "two pages max" means using both pages when the record fills them. In testing, a 1,283-word resume cut to 690 words was rejected as having lost too much of the original.

**Design.** Typeset the PDF (LaTeX or similar): a word-processor build reads as a template. Keep it single column and text-based, with fonts embedded and hyphenation off so keywords stay whole. Build the PDF and the `.docx` from one source and one set of design tokens, so the two match.

**Machine-readable PDF.** Test the extracted text with several parsers (pdftotext, pdfminer, pdfplumber, pypdf), not just one. In testing, a XeLaTeX PDF read cleanly in pdftotext but failed in the others:
- it had no space characters between words, so pdfplumber fused them;
- letter-spaced headings came out as single letters;
- hyphens mapped to U+2010, so the phone number failed a regex.

pdfLaTeX with `\pdfinterwordspaceon`, `glyphtounicode` and ligatures off fixed all three. Don't letter-space headings.

**Word file.** This is the file to upload to application portals.
- Use real Heading 1 section titles and list numbering on every bullet paragraph.
- Write contact details as plain text: some parsers drop hyperlink runs.
- Put a space before each date tab, and use no no-break spaces.
- Embed any font that isn't a system font. If Office also offers it as a cloud font, embed a subset that keeps the original glyph IDs. In testing, a renumbered subset garbled Word's spacing.
- Leave a line or two of spare room on the last page, because Word on another machine can reflow.

**Usually fails by:**
- over-cutting the person's own record;
- a skills block that lists everything ever touched;
- a summary that describes a personality ("passionate", "results-driven") instead of a capability;
- duties instead of results;
- every bullet the same length and weight;
- dash-joined bullets that run three lines;
- internal jargon on an external version;
- a crammed first page and a thin second.

## Short story or creative piece

**Job:** an effect on the reader, not a message: what they should feel, and what state they end in (laughing, calm, unsettled). Write the effect into the brief. The cold read passes when the reader describes that effect without being told. For a piece read aloud, the persona is the person reading it out, and they report how the listeners would react.

| Criterion | A 5 looks like |
|---|---|
| Want and obstacle | A character wants something specific, and something stands in the way, by the end of the first page. |
| Stakes | What's lost if they fail is felt, not stated. |
| Specific detail | Concrete, sensory, surprising details. No generic adjectives. |
| Scene over summary | Key moments happen on the page, with action and dialogue. |
| Voice | Consistent and particular. Dialogue sounds like people talking. |
| Turn | At least one turn the reader didn't see coming but believes. |
| Ending | Earned by what came before, not explained or moralized. |

**Tests:** cut the first paragraph; it usually helps. Read it aloud. Does the title earn its place?

**For kids:** words they know, but never dumbed down. Dense, not empty; weird is good. Funny on purpose. Never spell out the moral. Fits a read-aloud length (five to ten minutes unless the brief says otherwise).

**Gates:** length. Fit for the audience's age.

**Usually fails by:** summarizing instead of dramatizing. Problems solved by luck. A moral stated at the end. Purple prose. Generic names and settings.

## Anything else

Use the universal rules and build the criteria from the brief: who the reader is, the one job, and what a 5 would look like for each part of it. Write those criteria into `brief.md` before drafting, so they can't drift to fit the draft.
